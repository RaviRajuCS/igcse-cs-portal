Lesson overview

Period 11 · Encryption

Topic 2 · Data Transmission: Students move from protecting the integrity of transmitted data to protecting its confidentiality by introducing encryption and the basic distinction between plaintext, ciphertext, encryption and decryption.

Success criteria: Students can explain why encryption is needed, distinguish plaintext from ciphertext, describe encryption and decryption, and explain the role of a key.

Prior learning: Periods 6–10 — errors and error detection.

Key vocabulary: encryption, decryption, plaintext, ciphertext, key, confidentiality, sender, receiver.

My teaching route

Change the question: “Is it correct?” → “Can others read it?”

  1. Retrieve error detection.
  2. Ask whether correct data is necessarily private.
  3. Introduce plaintext and ciphertext.
  4. Show encryption as a transformation.
  5. Introduce a key.
  6. Show decryption at the receiver.
  7. Distinguish encryption from error detection.
Teaching principle: Error detection checks whether data has been altered. Encryption protects the information from being understood by unauthorised people.
0–7 min · Retrieval

What problem did we solve?

  • Why can transmitted data contain errors?
  • What does parity check?
  • What does a checksum check?
  • What does an echo check do?
New question: Suppose the data arrives correctly. What if somebody else can read it while it is being transmitted?
7–16 min · Teach

Why encrypt data?

Data transmitted across a network may contain information that should not be understood by unauthorised people.

Encryption changes readable data into an encoded form so that it is difficult for an unauthorised person to understand.

Examples include passwords, personal information, financial information and confidential messages.

Plaintext is the original readable data.

Important: Encryption does not mean that the data has been deleted or destroyed. It has been transformed into another form.

The sender encrypts the original data. The encrypted data is transmitted. The receiver decrypts it to recover the original information.

Key idea: The encryption/decryption process uses a key. The key controls the transformation used by the encryption system.

Simple classroom cipher

Use a simple Caesar-style example only to demonstrate the idea of transformation. If each letter is shifted by 1:

CiphertextIFMMP

The receiver applies the reverse shift to decrypt the ciphertext.

This is a teaching example. Real encryption systems are much more sophisticated.

🎯 Learning goals

  • Explain why encryption is needed.
  • Define plaintext and ciphertext.
  • Explain encryption and decryption.
  • Explain the role of a key.
  • Distinguish encryption from error detection.

1 · Why encrypt?

Imagine sending a private message across a network. Even if the message reaches the correct receiver, an unauthorised person might try to intercept it.

Encryption protects confidentiality by transforming readable information into an encoded form.

2 · Plaintext

Plaintext is the original readable information before encryption.

Example: MEET AT 5 is plaintext because it can be read directly.

3 · Ciphertext

Ciphertext is the transformed information produced by encryption.

Example: MEET AT 5 may become a sequence that does not make sense to an unauthorised reader.

5 · What is a key?

A key is information used by an encryption system to control how the data is encrypted and/or decrypted.

Think of a key like a secret setting: the correct key allows an authorised user to perform the appropriate cryptographic operation.

6 · Interactive check

Which term means the original readable data before encryption?

Which term describes the encrypted form?

Which process converts ciphertext back into readable data?

7 · Simple cipher demonstration

For a simple classroom example, shift every letter forward by one position:

HELLO → IFMMP

To decrypt it, shift each letter backward by one:

IFMMP → HELLO

Real-world encryption is far more complex than this example.

8 · Encryption vs error detection

QuestionTechnique
“Has the data probably been altered?”Error detection
“Can an unauthorised person understand the data?”Encryption
Do not confuse them: encryption protects confidentiality; error detection checks integrity.

9 · Exam practice

Question: Describe how data can be encrypted before transmission and decrypted after it is received.

The original plaintext is encrypted using an encryption process and key to produce ciphertext. The ciphertext is transmitted to the receiver. The receiver uses the appropriate decryption process and key to recover the plaintext.

10 · Challenge

A message arrives at the correct destination without any transmission error. Why might encryption still have been necessary?

Because the data could have been intercepted while being transmitted. Encryption helps prevent an unauthorised person from understanding the information.

11 · Common mistakes

  • Calling plaintext the encrypted message.
  • Calling ciphertext the original readable data.
  • Thinking encryption detects transmission errors.
  • Thinking encryption and decryption are the same operation.
  • Assuming the simple Caesar example represents modern encryption.
