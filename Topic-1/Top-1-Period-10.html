My teaching route
Lesson overview

Period 10 · Number Systems Mastery

Purpose: A complete consolidation and assessment lesson for the Number Systems section of Topic 1. Students bring together binary, denary, hexadecimal, binary addition, overflow, logical shifts and two's complement.

Success criteria: Students can select the correct number-system method, perform conversions and calculations accurately, explain representation limits, and use appropriate Cambridge exam language.

Prior learning: Periods 1–9.

Key vocabulary: binary, denary, hexadecimal, conversion, addition, carry, overflow, logical shift, two's complement, signed, unsigned, range.

My teaching route

Before students enter

  • Tell students this is a consolidation lesson, not a new-content lesson.
  • Encourage students to show working for every conversion and calculation.
  • Use the diagnostic questions to identify misconceptions for individual follow-up.
  • Keep the key rules available, but gradually remove them during the assessment section.
Teacher principle: assess the student's choice of method as well as the final answer.
0–7 min · Retrieval

Number Systems Flash Recall

  1. What are the bases of binary, denary and hexadecimal?
  2. Which hexadecimal digits represent 10–15?
  3. What is the maximum unsigned 8-bit value?
  4. What is the 8-bit two's complement range?
  5. What does a left logical shift do?
7–15 min · Diagnostic

Choose the correct method

Ask students to identify the method before calculating:

  • 10110110₂ → denary
  • 174₁₀ → hexadecimal
  • 3A₁₆ → denary
  • 01010101₂ + 00100111₂
  • 200 + 70 using 8-bit unsigned binary
  • −13 → 8-bit two's complement
Teaching point: strong students recognise the representation and required process before they begin the arithmetic.
15–25 min · Core assessment

Conversions

  1. Convert 10110101₂ to denary.
  2. Convert 213₁₀ to hexadecimal.
  3. Convert 7C₁₆ to denary.
  4. Convert 94₁₀ to binary.
  5. Convert −27₁₀ to 8-bit two's complement.
  6. Convert 11100101₂ to denary as an 8-bit two's complement value.
Teacher check: students should show place values or conversion steps, not just answers.
25–34 min · Calculations

Binary addition, overflow and shifts

  1. 00110110₂ + 00010101₂
  2. 01111111₂ + 00000001₂
  3. 11001000₂ + 00101000₂ — decide whether overflow occurs in 8-bit unsigned representation.
  4. Shift 00110100₂ left by 2 places.
  5. Shift 01101000₂ right by 3 places.
Teacher check: distinguish the addition carry from overflow and require students to explain the range test.
34–41 min · Application

Explain, don't just calculate

  1. Explain why computers use binary.
  2. Explain why hexadecimal is useful to humans.
  3. Explain why an 8-bit unsigned result can overflow.
  4. Explain why two's complement is useful.
Exam language: “Explain” requires a reason or consequence, not merely a definition.
41–47 min · Mini-mock

Cambridge-style mixed questions

Q1. Convert 5D₁₆ to denary.

Q2. Convert 173₁₀ to hexadecimal.

Q3. Add 00101101₂ and 00010111₂.

Q4. State whether 250 + 20 produces overflow in 8-bit unsigned representation.

Q5. Give the 8-bit two's complement representation of −9.

Q6. Shift 00111000₂ right by 2 places.

47–50 min · Exit assessment

Mastery check

  1. One conversion you can now do confidently.
  2. One mistake you must avoid in binary calculations.
  3. One thing you still need to practise.
Teacher decision: Students who are secure can move to the next Data Representation area: text, including character sets such as ASCII and Unicode. Students needing support receive targeted number-system practice.

  1. 10110101₂ → denary
  2. 213₁₀ → hexadecimal
  3. 7C₁₆ → denary
  4. 94₁₀ → binary
  5. −27₁₀ → 8-bit two's complement

Answers:
1. 181
2. D5₁₆
3. 124
4. 01011110₂
5. 11100101₂
6. −27

2 · Binary addition

Complete:

00110110 + 00010101
01001011₂ = 75₁₀.

3 · Overflow

Does 250 + 20 overflow an 8-bit unsigned representation?

Reason: 250 + 20 = 270, which is greater than the 8-bit maximum of 255.

4 · Logical shifts

00111000₂ → left 2

01101000₂ → right 3

00111000 → 11100000.
01101000 → 00001101.

5 · Two's complement

Convert −9 into 8-bit two's complement.

9 = 00001001 → invert 11110110 → add 1 = 11110111.

6 · Explain

Write one or two sentences for each:

  1. Why do computers use binary?
  2. Why is hexadecimal useful?
  3. What is overflow?
  4. Why is two's complement needed?
Key points: binary matches two-state electronic switching; hexadecimal provides a compact human-readable representation of binary; overflow occurs when a result is outside the available fixed-width range; two's complement provides a representation for negative integers.

7 · Mini-mock

Answers: 93; AD₁₆; 01000100₂; Yes; 11110111₂; 00001110₂.

8 · Common exam mistakes

  • Using the wrong place-value system.
  • Forgetting hexadecimal A–F values.
  • Ignoring carries in binary addition.
  • Calling every carry overflow.
  • Using unsigned place values for two's complement.
  • Forgetting the fixed number of bits.
